Visitor electronic card spend in Queenstown Wānaka

That was 1% of international visitor electronic card spend, the 12th largest international origin. Cafes, Restaurants, Bars & Takeaways took the largest share (20%), and 71% of spend was in Queenstown.

Market context

Overview

Germany is Europe's largest economy and the world's third-largest by nominal GDP, underpinned by a strong industrial base, world-class engineering, and a highly skilled workforce. It is a federal parliamentary republic and a founding member of the European Union and NATO. Germany's social model, characterised by strong labour protections, a well-developed welfare state, and a culture of consensus-building, has historically supported both economic performance and social stability. However, Germany is navigating a period of structural transition: managing the phase-out of nuclear energy, reducing dependence on Russian gas following the 2022 energy shock, decarbonising heavy industry, and grappling with demographic decline. In 2025, the economy stabilised after two years of contraction, but the outlook remains cautious. A new federal government formed in early 2025 signalled a shift toward greater infrastructure investment and some easing of the debt brake, which may provide modest support to activity.

Economic

Germany's economy grew just 0.3% in 2025 in calendar-adjusted terms, extending its prolonged stagnation. The European Commission projects expansion of 0.6% in 2026 and 0.9% in 2027, supported by a ramp-up in public spending, though the German government's own forecast has been cut to 0.5% for 2026 following fresh geopolitical uncertainty. The biggest near-term shift is fiscal: the 2025 reform of the debt brake has turned fiscal policy clearly expansionary, with defence and infrastructure spending estimated to contribute 1.3 percentage points to GDP growth cumulatively by 2028. The deficit ratio is expected to rise from 2.7% of GDP in 2024 to around 4.8% by 2028. Inflation eased to 2.3% in 2025 and is projected to tick up to around 2.9% in 2026. Unemployment is rising modestly, forecast to reach 4.0% in 2026 as manufacturing job losses offset gains in public services. Manufacturing is stabilising after years of drag, though Chinese competition continues to weigh on exports. Core structural challenges remain: high energy costs, demographic-driven labour shortages, slow digital adoption, and the automotive sector's ongoing transition.

Society & Culture

With a population of approximately 84.4 million, Germany is the most populous EU member state. Population growth in recent years has been driven almost entirely by net immigration, primarily from EU countries, Ukraine, and other global flows, contributing to increasing ethnic and cultural diversity. Social cohesion is under pressure from economic insecurity, housing shortages, the challenge of integrating large numbers of newcomers, and growing political polarisation, reflected in rising support for the far-right AfD in state and federal elections. The population is ageing rapidly: by the mid-2030s, those aged 67 and over will constitute a significantly larger share of the population while the working-age cohort contracts. This demographic profile puts long-term pressure on pension systems, healthcare, and labour supply. Immigration is increasingly seen as a partial structural remedy, but effective integration in language, education, and employment remains a significant challenge. Germany has deep cultural strengths: a globally influential tradition in music, philosophy, literature, and design, alongside world-class research institutions and a pragmatic civic culture.

Environment

Germany has made substantial progress on climate, cutting GHG emissions by approximately 46% below 1990 levels by 2023, largely through coal phase-out and the economic slowdown of recent years. The country targets climate neutrality by 2045, with a 65% emissions cut by 2030 under the updated Climate Change Act. The Energiewende aims for 80% renewable electricity by 2030, with wind and solar capacity expanding rapidly. Germany is on track to phase out coal entirely by 2038. Key challenges include decarbonising transport, buildings, and energy-intensive industry. Air quality has improved markedly, though biodiversity loss in agricultural landscapes and water quality in some catchments remain serious concerns. Germany faces planning and permitting bottlenecks for infrastructure, grid expansion, and renewable energy deployment. Balancing industrial competitiveness with the costs of the energy transition is a defining economic and political challenge for the decade ahead.

About DQ and LWT

Destination Queenstown (DQ) and Lake Wānaka Tourism (LWT) are the Regional Tourism Organisations (RTOs) responsible for destination marketing, both domestically and internationally, and destination management, delivering selected initiatives from the Queenstown Wānaka destination management plan.

The RTOs' role is to market Queenstown and Wānaka as leading tourism destinations. This focuses on attracting high contributing visitors and supporting delivery of the destination management plan to ensure a resilient and future-focused industry, within a thriving community and environment.

The RTO strategy has evolved to support the goals of Travel to a Thriving Future, as well as continuing to support the visitor economy with values-aligned marketing, product development, and capability building in line with a regenerative tourism future.
